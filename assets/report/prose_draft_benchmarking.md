## 11.x Measured Performance from Session Telemetry

The performance claims summarized in Section 11 are supplemented here by direct measurement against the platform's own telemetry record. Every session run since 2026-04-10 has been captured by the 10 Hz JSONL session logger in `braccio_main_runner/session_logs/`. The resulting archive contains 104 session files spanning five calendar days and 187,726 telemetry frames, which is a sample size large enough to characterize the latency distribution rather than only its worst case.

Of the 104 captured sessions, 57 contained at least one obstacle-triggered state transition, yielding 468 discrete detection events in total. The telemetry sample cadence was confirmed to be tight: the median inter-frame interval was 100 milliseconds with a p95 of 100 milliseconds and a p99 of 101 milliseconds, matching the designed 10 Hz tick rate of the host controller.

**Detection-source distribution.** The 468 obstacle events were attributed by the safety stack to their triggering sensor channel as follows: CH1 (back-facing ToF) was the dominant channel with 143 events (30.6%), CH0 (front-facing ToF) produced 130 events (27.8%), and CH2 (top/side ToF, promoted from advisory to replan authority on 2026-04-10) accounted for 127 events (27.1%). The bottom-facing CH3 channel contributed 55 events (11.8%), and the four infrared sensors together triggered 13 events (2.8%). The near-parity between CH0, CH1, and CH2 confirms that the channel-promotion decision recovered a previously silenced third of all detections.

**Per-channel detection distance.** The median distance at which each channel triggered a transition away from the clear state was 105 mm for CH0, 94 mm for CH1, 98 mm for CH2, and 47 mm for CH3. The upper three channels cluster around 100 mm, consistent with their common 250 mm threshold and the practical range at which obstacles are first reliably resolved in the 8×8 VL53L5CX grid. CH3's lower median reflects its proximity to the workbench surface and its use as a short-range floor-clearance channel rather than as a primary obstacle sensor.

**Response-kind distribution.** Of the 468 events, 301 (64.3%) resulted in a `replan` response, 154 (32.9%) resulted in a `warn` response that did not immediately interrupt the commanded trajectory, and 13 (2.8%) escalated to a `back_away` response. The dominance of `replan` indicates that the majority of detected obstacles were close enough to block the intended trajectory but not so close as to require the arm to reverse along its current path.

**Reaction latency.** Two latency metrics were computed over the event set. The first is the behavior-tree response latency, defined as the interval between an `obstacle_response` state transition event and the next behavior-tree mode change. This metric captures the time required for the planning tier to commit to a reaction strategy. Its median value across 229 measurable events was 804 milliseconds, with a p95 of 1.81 seconds and a p99 of 1.88 seconds. The second metric is the arm-motion halt latency, defined as the interval between an `obstacle_response` transition and the first telemetry frame in which the measured joint deltas fell below a 0.25-degree threshold across all six joints. This metric captures the reactive-safety halt response and is the more relevant figure for collision-avoidance guarantees. Across 401 measurable events the median halt latency was 170 milliseconds, the p95 was 1.14 seconds, and the p99 was 1.65 seconds. Figure N (histogram) presents the full distribution of both metrics.

The gap between the two metrics reflects the separation between reactive safety and deliberative planning in the system's design. The capsule-collision gate in `SafetyAPI.plan_and_validate` runs synchronously on the 10 Hz host tick and produces a halt command within one to two ticks of detection, which accounts for the 170 millisecond halt median. The behavior-tree mode change carries the full replan-validate-dispatch cycle, including BiRRT path search and, when recovery is needed, the six-strategy replanner ladder described in Section 8.3.2; this accounts for the 804 millisecond planning median. The safety-critical halt path is deliberately shorter than the deliberative replan path, so that the arm stops first and plans second.

**Caveats.** The retrospective nature of the measurement introduces two known limitations. First, the halt-latency metric detects motion cessation through joint-position telemetry at 10 Hz, so the true halt is completed some fraction of a tick before it is first observed in the log. The reported figures therefore upper-bound the true halt latency by up to one tick (100 milliseconds) in the worst case. Second, sessions without obstacle events contribute to the sample frame count but not to the event count; the detection coverage percentage cannot be computed from telemetry alone without a ground-truth record of every obstacle placed in the arm's workspace, which was not systematically captured. A controlled benchmarking protocol with instrumented ground-truth obstacle placement is identified in Section 14 as a direction for follow-on work.
